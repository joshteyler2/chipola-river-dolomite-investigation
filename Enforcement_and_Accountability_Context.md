# Enforcement and accountability context

This document separates verified institutional context from site-specific proof. Statewide staffing and enforcement trends can help explain regulatory capacity; they do not prove that any individual Dolomite or Arcosa decision was improper.

## The Dolomite settlement was not forced by a $10,000 total ceiling

The $9,500 Dolomite consent order was not constrained by a $10,000 administrative ceiling. The applicable ceiling in 2024 was higher.

- Section 403.121, Florida Statutes, allowed administrative penalties up to **$50,000 per assessment** by the time of the June 2024 order. The Department retains judicial authority above that amount and for other remedies.
- FDEP’s current [Directive 923](sources/39-FDEP-Directive-923-Settlement-Guidelines-2024.pdf), effective October 30, 2024, implements the $50,000 cap: when scheduled penalties plus adjustments exceed $50,000, a district may cap at $50,000 for the administrative route or pursue judicial relief. It also contemplates upward adjustments for economic benefit and qualifying noncompliance history, and peer/Deputy Secretary review above $25,000.
- Older FDEP material showing a $10,000 ceiling predates the statutory increase and should not be used to explain a 2024 order.

The Dolomite order itself assesses $9,000 in civil penalties—two $4,500 scheduled items—plus $500 costs. That shows the schedule used; it does not establish that no discretion existed. To evaluate discretion, request the penalty worksheet, economic-benefit calculation, history adjustment, harm/deviation rating, authorization review, settlement communications, and any reason for omitting or reducing adjustments.

Official source: [§403.121, Florida Statutes](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0403/Sections/0403.121.html).

## Statewide enforcement trend

The July 2026 PEER/Waterkeepers Florida report, documented with page locators and its official link in [source 40](sources/40-PEER-Waterkeepers-Florida-2025-Enforcement-Report-Notes.md), compiles FDEP enforcement data and reports:

| Period/measure | Reported value |
|---|---:|
| 2024 actions | 699 |
| 2025 actions | 755 |
| 1996–2010 annual average | 1,303 |
| 2011–2018 annual average | 431 |
| 2019–2025 annual average | 654 |

The year-over-year increase is real, and the longer-run level remains far below the pre-2011 average. PEER and Waterkeepers are advocacy organizations; their report is valuable trend evidence but is not an adjudication of Dolomite or Arcosa compliance. A separate PEER report says FDEP initiated zero mining enforcement cases in 2017. That is a historical statewide program statistic, not evidence that mining enforcement is literally zero today.

FDEP’s counterpoint deserves inclusion: the state says it has dedicated more than **$3.3 billion** since 2019 to water resources and more than 1,000 projects. Restoration investment and enforcement activity measure different things. The investment is substantial; it does not by itself answer whether permit oversight and deterrence are adequate.

Sources: [PEER 2025 enforcement report page](https://peer.org/report-2025-florida-enforcement-report/), [PEER 2017 enforcement report page](https://peer.org/florida-racks-up-second-worst-eco-enforcement-in-30-years/), and [FDEP/Protecting Florida Together BMAP investment page](https://protectingfloridatogether.gov/ConsensusDocument/BMAPs).

## Staffing capacity

The Environmental Integrity Project’s December 2025 multistate report, documented with page locators and its official link in [source 41](sources/41-EIP-State-of-Decline-2025-Notes.md), reports Florida pollution-control staffing falling from **2,096 in FY2010 to 1,702 in FY2024**: 394 positions, or 19 percent.

Two precision corrections matter:

- Florida had the largest **absolute number** of eliminated positions in the report.
- North Carolina had the largest **percentage** decline, 32 percent. Calling Florida’s 19 percent the largest percentage decline is wrong.

This supports a general capacity concern. It does not prove how many inspectors were assigned to these mines, that a particular inspection was “the first eyes in months,” or that experienced biologists were replaced on the 2012 Dolomite study. Those site/personnel questions require rosters, position histories, assignments, calendars, and supervisory records.

## Safe Waterways Act: important, but narrower than a general pollution-notice law

The 2024 Legislature passed CS/CS/HB 165 unanimously; Governor DeSantis vetoed it on June 26, 2024. The enrolled bill, preserved as [source 42](sources/42-Florida-HB165-2024-Enrolled.pdf), focused on **bacteriological contamination of beach waters and public bathing places**. It would have required sampling procedures, notification after a failed sample, health advisories/closures, signage, and related agency action, including investigation of wastewater facilities within one mile in specified circumstances.

It was not a universal requirement for immediate public notice of every toxicant, wildlife mortality, or contamination event in every Florida waterbody. Existing Florida pollution-notice and State Watch Office systems still apply to reportable releases. The veto is relevant to transparency policy, but it does not by itself explain why no notice exists for this reported Chipola event.

Official sources: [Florida Senate bill history](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2024/165) and [Governor’s veto announcement](https://flgov.com/eog/news/press/2024/governor-ron-desantis-vetoes-four-bills).

## Public contracts: a real local relationship, not evidence of favoritism

The City of Marianna awarded Baxter’s Asphalt & Concrete the Old Cottondale Road roadway/water-system project in April 2021 for **$1,239,678.96**. The official award says Baxter was the lowest overall qualified bidder among four contractors. The same packet lists multiple smaller street-overlay jobs performed by Baxter. These records establish that Baxter is a recurring local public-works contractor; they do not establish improper procurement or regulatory influence.

The available county procurement archive did not permit a complete historical vendor-total search. A Chapter 119 request should seek Jackson County vendor payments, purchase orders, bids, awards, continuing-service agreements, emergency work, change orders, and material purchases for Baxter/Dolomite and name variants. ## Outstanding Florida Water antidegradation: the strongest records lever

The Chipola reach is an Outstanding Florida Water. Rule 62-4.242 requires a qualifying applicant to demonstrate public interest and protection of existing ambient water quality, subject to the rule’s specific exceptions and renewal provisions. “Existing ambient” is not simply the numeric Class III floor; the rule uses the better of designated criteria or applicable baseline ambient quality, with treatment of natural cyclic variation.

The practical questions are:

- What baseline period and stations were used for each facility?
- Did the analysis include the other aggregate operation and existing upstream loading?
- What hydrologic, groundwater, swallet, tributary, storm-event, and mixing assumptions were used?
- Was any renewal or modification treated as exempt because it caused no new or increased discharge?
- What evidence supports no lowering of existing ambient quality after construction changes and newly documented connections?

Arcosa’s 2019 permit does contain a Level I WQBEL and antidegradation analysis; it cannot fairly be described as having no analysis. But that analysis relied on the then-described swallet/tributary geometry, and FDEP documented a changed/natural connection in 2024. Dolomite’s current analysis relies heavily on 2012 field work that did not bracket both operations. A new reach-scale cumulative demonstration is therefore a strong, records-grounded request—not a foregone legal conclusion.

Official sources: [Rule 62-4.242](https://flrules.org/gateway/ruleno.asp?Section=0&id=62-4.242), [FDEP OFW overview](https://floridadep.gov/dear/water-quality-standards/content/outstanding-florida-waters), [Arcosa 2019 permit](sources/33-FDEP-2019-Arcosa-North-Florida-Rock-NPDES-FL0980722.pdf), and [Arcosa 2024 inspection](sources/35-FDEP-2024-Arcosa-Compliance-Assistance-and-Inspection.pdf).

## Bottom line

The record supports concern about low penalties, repeated after-the-fact authorization, reduced monitoring, thin public data, and a statewide decline in enforcement capacity. It also supplies ordinary structural explanations—program silos, scheduled administrative penalties, forward-looking permit standards, settlement practice, and staffing loss. There is no reviewed evidence of bribery, quid pro quo, or site-specific political intervention. The next defensible step is targeted disclosure and independent sampling, not an accusation ahead of the evidence.
